Future Trends: AI, Mobility & the Wellness Economy

An institutional perspective on structural shifts reshaping capital allocation, real assets, and community-scale development over the coming decades.

Trend 1: AI and Robotics as a Structural Productivity Shock

Artificial intelligence and robotics are approaching a phase of rapid deployment across the global economy. Current projections suggest AI could account for approximately 2.7% of global GDP by 2034, while a meaningful share of world production is already robotized. As systems approach greater autonomy—including scenarios in which advanced robotics can design and assemble further capacity—labour markets may experience a structural rebalancing in which traditional employment becomes optional for a larger portion of the population. The resulting surplus of discretionary time is likely to redirect demand toward health, experience, place, and community rather than pure consumption of goods.

Trend 2: The Rise of Global Nomadism and Population Mobility

Digital nomad populations are estimated near 60 million today and, under broader definitions that include economic migrants and climate-displaced populations, could approach one billion by mid-century. Mobility is increasing both out of necessity—remote work, climate pressure, demographic concentration, and labour arbitrage—and out of possibility, as healthcare, education, relationship formation, and professional activity become more digitally portable. This shift has direct implications for residential demand, hospitality, and place-based infrastructure outside traditional urban cores.

Trend 3: Alternative Living Models and Place-Based Platforms

Evidence of this transition is already visible in policy and capital markets: nomad visa regimes, private city initiatives, retreat and regenerative village concepts, and community-centric living platforms. Precedents such as WeWork’s community-plus-workplace model, and subsequent attempts to brand and scale residential community as a product (including large-scale venture-backed living platforms), illustrate investor appetite for formats that combine real estate, services, and social infrastructure.

Thesis: A Global Wellness Revolution

As discretionary time expands and professional identity becomes less tightly coupled to a single workplace, capital and consumer behaviour are expected to reallocate toward wellness—longevity science, nutrition, contemplative practice, nature reconnection, and “conscious” experiential formats. Simultaneously, the limits of a pure productivity narrative are becoming clearer: developed economies have delivered substantially higher GDP since the 1990s while also recording elevated rates of depression and related mental-health burdens. The working hypothesis is that optimal outcomes combine the best of technology with the conditions under which humans evolved to thrive—community, nature, and purposeful activity—rather than maximizing hours worked alone.

Additional hypotheses relevant to real assets and community development include:

  • Large urban cores are increasingly overpriced relative to the lifestyle value they deliver once remote access to work, healthcare, education, and social networks is feasible.
  • Longevity “blue zones” are characterized less by isolated biohacks than by durable belonging within a coherent community.
  • There is a material, if under-measured, correlation between weak community health and outcomes ranging from depression and anxiety to psychosomatic illness and cardiovascular risk.
  • In an economy where routine cognitive and physical tasks are automated, human economic contribution concentrates in creativity—and creative capacity is maximized when individuals are well, generating a second-order productivity dividend from wellness infrastructure.

Implications for Capital, Policy, and Development

Taken together, these trends support a view that wellness is not a niche consumer category but a multi-trillion-dollar structural market. Within that market, scalable models of healthy community—integrating housing, services, nature, and social design—represent a distinctive opportunity for institutional capital, landowners, and policymakers. Realizing that opportunity requires bridging disciplines that rarely share a common table: anthropology and behavioural science, real-estate and infrastructure finance, and land ownership and master planning.

Parallel advances in digitised services—particularly education, healthcare, relationship platforms, and work—reduce the friction of locating outside traditional metro centres. Over time, this may support the emergence of community development as a more formal applied social science, with measurable metrics, underwriting frameworks, and investable product structures suited to long-duration capital.
